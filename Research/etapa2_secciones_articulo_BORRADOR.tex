\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Data and splits}
We use the public battle logs of \cite{angliss2026} for the Gen 9 Champions VGC 2026 Regulation M-A format
(file \texttt{logs\_gen9championsvgc2026regma.json}, fixed revision). After parsing, 6{,}902 of 6{,}905 battles are valid.
To avoid leakage, the data are split by date and by Bo3 series (a series never crosses splits):
train (4{,}831 battles, 11-04 to 25-05-2026), validation (1{,}036, 25-05 to 05-06) and test (1{,}035, 05-06 to 20-06).
For evaluation we freeze 10 validation and 20 test team pairs; no team (species set) in validation or test appears in train,
and test shares no team with validation. The parser was checked against 20 hand-annotated training replays
(leads and revealed reserves), with full agreement. Test was evaluated exactly once, after the protocol was committed.

\subsection{Decision problem}
Each player chooses a bring $b=(p,r)$: a lead pair $p$ (15 options) and a reserve pair $r$ from the remaining four (6 options),
for 90 brings. We model the choice as a two-step episode with terminal reward $R\in\{0,1\}$ (win) and $\gamma=1$;
the in-battle policy $\pi_b$ is a fixed heuristic, identical for both sides and all methods
(\texttt{SimpleHeuristicsPlayer} with automatic Mega Evolution, because the original player never Mega Evolves).
Logs only reveal the leads and the Pokémon that entered, so the true bring is known up to the consistent set
$\mathcal C(o)$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:consistent}.

\subsection{Policy and behaviour cloning}
Each own Pokémon $x$ is described by a hand-crafted feature vector $\phi_x$ computed against the full rival team:
train log-frequencies of being lead, reserve or left out, normalised usage, offensive and defensive type matchups
against the six rivals, and a species one-hot (\texttt{<UNK>} if unseen). The policy is an autoregressive linear softmax,
\begin{equation}
\pi_\theta(p,r\mid s)=\operatorname{softmax}_p\Big(\sum_{x\in p}\theta_L^\top\phi_x\Big)\cdot
\operatorname{softmax}_{r\mid p}\Big(\sum_{x\in r}\theta_R^\top\phi_x\Big),
\end{equation}
with illegal options masked. We chose a linear model over a Transformer or DeepSets encoder because there are
about 9.7k training examples, the gradient can be written in closed form and checked by finite differences,
and the result is easier to inspect; the cost is that interactions between Pokémon are only additive.
The behaviour-cloning policy $\rho_{BC}$ is trained on both sides of every train battle with the partial-label loss
of Eq.~\eqref{eq:partial} (Adam, $\eta=0.01$, L2 $10^{-4}$, early stopping on validation), with 3 seeds.

\subsection{Reinforcement learning agent}
The agent is initialised from $\rho_{BC}$ and trained with REINFORCE using the batch-mean return as baseline,
an entropy bonus and a KL penalty towards $\rho_{BC}$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L(\theta)=-\mathbb E\big[(R-\bar R_{\text{batch}})\log\pi_\theta(b\mid s)\big]
-\beta_H\,\mathcal H[\pi_\theta]+\beta_{KL}\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta\,\|\,\rho_{BC}).
\end{equation}
Episodes are two steps long with a terminal reward, so the Monte Carlo return is exact and unbiased; bootstrapped
methods (DQN, A2C) add no benefit and we use neither a replay buffer nor a target network.
The KL term is meant to keep the agent close to human brings and limit exploitation of $\pi_b$.
Training runs on the 200 train pairs against a stochastic Usage rival in Pokémon Showdown,
50{,}000 episodes per seed, 3 seeds, $\beta_H=0.01$, $\beta_{KL}=0.1$, Adam $\eta=0.003$ with gradient clipping at 1.
The checkpoint is selected by validation win rate and evaluated greedily.

\subsection{Baselines}
Random (uniform over legal brings), Usage-argmax (most frequent train bring, the literature baseline),
Tipos (type-matchup heuristic), BC (ablation: does RL add anything?) and a planner that maximises the
bilinear win-probability model $\hat v$ of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:bilinear}--\eqref{eq:marginal} over the 90 brings.

\section{Experiments and results}\label{sec:exp}

\subsection{Protocol and preregistered rule}
Every method plays the stochastic Usage rival on each pair for 1{,}000 battles (500 per side) in test
(200 in validation). Per pair we report the win rate with a Wilson 95\% interval; across pairs we average over seeds
and report the mean with a $t$ 95\% interval (unit = pair) and the standard deviation across seeds.
The preregistered rule (committed before test) says the agent \emph{beats 55\%} only if the lower bound of the
$t$ interval over the 20 test pairs exceeds 0.55; if only the mean does, the result is \emph{compatible but not conclusive}.
Paired differences against BC, Usage-argmax, Tipos and Random are reported as secondary, descriptive comparisons.
All 12 test evaluations completed (20{,}000 battles each, no draws or timeouts).

The agent wins 59.5\% of its battles against Usage on unseen team pairs, with a lower $t$ bound of 0.561, so it
\emph{beats 55\%} under the preregistered rule (Table~\ref{tab:test}). Results are stable across seeds
(standard deviation 0.013).

The agent is clearly better than Random and better than Usage-argmax (Table~\ref{tab:diff}).
RL adds about 3.4 points over its BC initialisation, but this gain is at the edge of what 20 pairs can resolve:
the interval only just excludes zero, and with a $t_{19}$ multiplier it includes it.
The agent is not distinguishable from the type-matchup heuristic, and BC and Tipos are equal ($+0.000\pm0.031$).
The planner falls below BC ($-0.055\pm0.035$): with validation accuracy 0.548 and AUC 0.571, $\hat v$ is too weak to
act as a fitness function. These $\hat v$ figures are not directly comparable with Oblander's 0.664 / 0.730, because
the data, format and labels differ.

\subsection{Predicting human brings}
On the 2{,}070 player brings in test, $\rho_{BC}$ predicts the exact lead pair 14.4\% of the time (chance 6.67\%)
and a full bring consistent with the observation 4.6\% of the time (chance 1.11\%), with negligible variation
across seeds. Behaviour cloning captures part of the human choice, but most brings remain unpredicted.

\subsection{Exploration and exploitation}
We compared $\beta_H=0.01$ (3 seeds) with $\beta_H=0$ (seed 0) on the validation curves.
At about 15k episodes, validation win rate was 0.539 with $\beta_H=0.01$ and 0.525 with $\beta_H=0$ for the same seed;
the best checkpoints reached 0.616 and 0.623. One validation evaluation has a standard error of about 0.04, so neither
difference is measurable. Policy entropy was almost identical (4.14 vs.\ 4.12 at 15k, about 4.0 at the end, against a
maximum of $\ln 90\approx4.50$): at this weight the entropy bonus is small next to the policy gradient and the KL term,
and the policy stays broad during training even though it is evaluated greedily.
$\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta\|\rho_{BC})$ grew from about 0.02 at 15k to 0.09--0.11 at 50k, so the agent drifts only
moderately from the human prior.

\subsection{Discussion}
On validation (10 pairs), Usage-argmax scored below Random (0.467 vs.\ 0.553), which suggested that human brings
transfer poorly to the heuristic player. This did not replicate on test (0.531 vs.\ 0.492), so we treat it as noise
from the small validation set rather than as evidence of distribution shift. Absolute levels also moved between
splits (BC rose from 0.484 to 0.561), which is why we rely on paired differences on the same pairs.

The main result answers the research question under a strong condition: both sides use the same fixed in-battle
policy $\pi_b$. A bring that wins under $\pi_b$ may exploit its weaknesses instead of being good in human play, and
the KL penalty only partly guards against this. The fact that a simple type-matchup heuristic performs as well as
the agent points the same way: much of the measurable advantage may come from type coverage that $\pi_b$ uses well.
Etapa 3 re-evaluates the agent with a second in-battle policy (MaxBasePower) to test this.

\subsection{Limitations}
A single in-battle policy and a single rival; a linear policy with additive interactions; three seeds and 20 test
pairs; deterministic baselines evaluated once; checkpoint selection on validation; an exploration sweep with one seed;
and diagnostics that measure imitation of human brings rather than the agent's quality.

\section{Conclusions}
Under a fixed heuristic battle policy, a REINFORCE agent initialised from behaviour cloning selects brings that win
59.5\% of battles against a usage-based rival on unseen teams, meeting the preregistered 55\% threshold.
It clearly outperforms random and usage-based brings, improves modestly and only marginally over its behaviour-cloning
initialisation, and does not outperform a simple type-matchup heuristic. A learned win-probability model of the
survey's bilinear form was not accurate enough to plan with. The next stage tests whether these brings remain good
under a different battle policy, which is the condition for claiming that the agent learned good brings rather than
weaknesses of the simulator's heuristic.
