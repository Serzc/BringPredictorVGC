\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Data and splits}
We use the public battle logs of \cite{angliss2026} for the Gen 9 Champions VGC 2026 Regulation M-A format
(file \texttt{logs\_gen9championsvgc2026regma.json}, fixed revision). After parsing, 6{,}902 of 6{,}905 battles are valid.
To avoid leakage, the data are split by date and by Bo3 series (a series never crosses splits):
train (4{,}831 battles, 11-04 to 25-05-2026), validation (1{,}036, 25-05 to 05-06) and test (1{,}035, 05-06 to 20-06).
For evaluation we freeze 10 validation and 20 test team pairs; no team (species set) in validation or test appears in train,
and test shares no team with validation. Test is read only once (Sec.~\ref{sec:exp}).

\subsection{Decision problem}
Each player chooses a bring $b=(p,r)$: a lead pair $p$ (15 options) and a reserve pair $r$ from the remaining four (6 options),
for 90 brings. We model the choice as a two-step episode with terminal reward $R\in\{0,1\}$ (win) and $\gamma=1$;
the in-battle policy $\pi_b$ is a fixed heuristic, identical for both sides and all methods
(\texttt{SimpleHeuristicsPlayer} with automatic Mega Evolution, because the fork's player never Mega Evolves).
Logs only reveal the leads and the Pokémon that entered, so the true bring is known up to the consistent set
$\mathcal C(o)$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:consistent}.

\subsection{Policy and behaviour cloning}
Each own Pokémon $x$ is described by a hand-crafted feature vector $\phi_x$ computed against the full rival team:
train log-frequencies of being lead, reserve or left out, normalised usage, offensive and defensive type matchups
against the six rivals, and a species one-hot (\texttt{<UNK>} if unseen). The policy is an autoregressive linear softmax,
\begin{equation}
\pi_\theta(p,r\mid s)=\operatorname{softmax}_p\Big(\sum_{x\in p}\theta_L^\top\phi_x\Big)\cdot
\operatorname{softmax}_{r\mid p}\Big(\sum_{x\in r}\theta_R^\top\phi_x\Big),
\end{equation}
with illegal options masked. We chose a linear model over a Transformer or DeepSets encoder because there are
about 9.7k training examples, the gradient can be written in closed form and checked by finite differences,
and the result is easier to inspect; the cost is that interactions between Pokémon are only additive.
The behaviour-cloning policy $\rho_{BC}$ is trained on both sides of every train battle with the partial-label loss
of Eq.~\eqref{eq:partial} (Adam, $\eta=0.01$, L2 $10^{-4}$, early stopping on validation), with 3 seeds.

\subsection{Reinforcement learning agent}
The agent is initialised from $\rho_{BC}$ and trained with REINFORCE using the batch-mean return as baseline,
an entropy bonus and a KL penalty towards $\rho_{BC}$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L(\theta)=-\mathbb E\big[(R-\bar R_{\text{batch}})\log\pi_\theta(b\mid s)\big]
-\beta_H\,\mathcal H[\pi_\theta]+\beta_{KL}\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta\,\|\,\rho_{BC}).
\end{equation}
Episodes are two steps long with a terminal reward, so the Monte Carlo return is exact and unbiased; bootstrapped
methods (DQN, A2C) add no benefit and we use neither a replay buffer nor a target network.
The KL term is meant to keep the agent close to human brings and limit exploitation of $\pi_b$.
Training runs on the 200 train pairs against a stochastic Usage rival in Pokémon Showdown,
50{,}000 episodes per seed, 3 seeds, $\beta_H=0.01$, $\beta_{KL}=0.1$, Adam $\eta=0.003$ with gradient clipping at 1.
The checkpoint is selected by validation win rate.

\subsection{Baselines}
Random (uniform over legal brings), Usage-argmax (most frequent train bring, the literature baseline),
Tipos (type-matchup heuristic), BC (ablation: does RL add anything?) and a planner that maximises the
bilinear win-probability model $\hat v$ of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:bilinear}--\eqref{eq:marginal} over the 90 brings.

\section{Experiments and results}\label{sec:exp}

\subsection{Protocol and preregistered rule}
Every method plays the stochastic Usage rival on each pair for 1{,}000 battles (500 per side) in test
(200 in validation). Per pair we report the win rate with a Wilson 95\% interval; across pairs we average over seeds
and report the mean with a $t$ 95\% interval (unit = pair) and the standard deviation across seeds.
The preregistered rule (committed before test) says the agent \emph{beats 55\%} only if the lower bound of the
$t$ interval over the 20 test pairs exceeds 0.55; if only the mean does, the result is \emph{compatible but not conclusive}.
Paired differences against BC, Usage-argmax, Tipos and Random are reported as secondary comparisons.

RL improves on BC by about 0.10 in all three seeds, with $\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta\|\rho_{BC})$ between 0.01 and 0.10

$\hat v$ reaches 0.548 accuracy and 0.571 AUC on validation; these are not directly comparable with
Oblander's 0.664 / 0.730, because the data, format and labels differ.
BC predicts the human lead pair 16\% of the time (chance 6.67\%).

\subsection{Discussion and limitations}
Under a fixed $\pi_b$, Usage-argmax scores below Random. This is a distribution shift: human brings were chosen for
human play, not for the heuristic player. Beating 55\% against Usage therefore does not by itself show that the agent
picks good brings, and on validation the agent does not beat the Tipos heuristic. The agent may be exploiting
weaknesses of $\pi_b$; Etapa 3 re-evaluates it with a second in-battle policy (MaxBasePower).
Other limitations: a single rival, a linear policy, three seeds, and 20 test pairs.
